## BLOCK 04 — THE STARTING POINT

### Framing principle

Critique the artefact, never the author. You don't know who made these — it may have been the founder, a relative, a print shop, or an agency. Every sentence below judges the file, not a person, and every direction gets credit before criticism. That's not just decency; it's what makes the analysis read as professional rather than as a designer showing off.

**Never write:** "their old logo was bad," "amateur," "whoever made this."
**Always write:** what the direction was trying to do, what worked, what broke.

### Copy  *(~110 words)*

```
Diwa arrived with three directions on the table and no agreement
about which one was the brand.

Each had something in it. Capitalising both D and A gave the
wordmark a rhythm worth keeping. The wave lines genuinely
gestured at moving air. The three-blade form fused two ideas —
fan blade and leaf — into a single shape, which is exactly the
kind of thinking a mark needs.

The problem was execution against the product. Photographic and
gradient elements don't survive one-colour printing, and this
brand prints on white plastic, brown cardboard, and adhesive
tape. And across the three, the same idea — moving air — was
being stated three different ways at once.

My job was to keep what was working and build it into one object.
```

**Highlight phrase:** *keep what was working and build it into one object.*

**Use your own critique slides as the images**, not pages from the brief. They're your layout, your annotations, your analysis — a meaningfully different artefact from the client's document, and the one you have the strongest claim to.

**Design notes:** WORKS column headed in `#2FA86A`, BREAKS in muted secondary text — not red. Red reads as an attack. Muted reads as an assessment.

### The one line that makes this block work

Close it with the sentence above: *"My job was to keep what was working and build it into one object."*

It reframes everything preceding it from teardown to synthesis, and it sets up the D-form — which literally does keep the airflow gesture and the leaf, just built into the letterform instead of pasted beside it.

## BLOCK 12A — BEFORE ↔ AFTER SLIDER

### Setup
```
Left    The strongest of the three prior directions
Right   diwa_primary_fullmark
```

Use their best one, not their weakest. Comparing against the strongest available option is more convincing and more honest — and it means nobody can accuse you of stacking the deck.

Both on white, same canvas width, marks optically matched in size rather than mathematically matched. Draggable divider, tap-to-toggle on mobile.

### Caption
```
Same ideas — water, air, growth. One of them is a single object
that holds at 20 pixels.
```

### Second slider, if you want it

More persuasive than the colour comparison, and completely uncontroversial since it's a technical demonstration:

```
Left    Prior direction, converted to flat single colour
Right   New mark, flat single colour
Caption Both marks reduced to one flat colour. This is the test
        every logo has to pass before anything else, because it's
        how the brand prints on cardboard and tape.
```

Showing the *test* rather than the *verdict* lets the reader reach the conclusion themselves. That's always stronger than telling them.

## RISK REDUCERS

Small choices that lower exposure without weakening the block:

1. **Your slides, not their brief pages.** Never reproduce a page carrying the "Confidential" stamp. If the stamp is visible in any export, that image doesn't go up.
2. **No provenance.** Don't say where the directions came from, who supplied them, or what document they were in. "Diwa arrived with three directions" is all the reader needs.
3. **No named author.** You don't know who made them. Don't speculate.
4. **Credit first, always.** Every direction gets its WORKS column. This is the single biggest difference between a critique that reads as professional and one that reads as a designer dunking on someone.
5. **Send the heads-up.** One message, framed as a notification rather than a request. It converts this from something that could surprise Didier into something he already knows about.
6. **Offer the page first.** "Happy to send you the page before it goes up" costs nothing and makes it nearly impossible for this to become a problem.

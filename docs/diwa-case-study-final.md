## GROUND RULES APPLIED

**Removed:** the audit block, the before/after slider, all images of previous logo directions, everything sourced from the creative brief.

**Do not, anywhere on the page:** show the earlier marks, itemise their flaws, quote the brief, or describe what was wrong with them in specifics. "It was cluttered / it used photographic elements / there were three competing versions" all reveal the contents of a confidential document.

**Safe to say:** what Diwa does, who the founder is, the awards, the patent, the product range, the market — all of it is on their site, in the press, or in their public dossier. And all of your own work, without limit.

**The one line you can safely use about scope:** *"Diwa came to me to rework their visual identity."* That describes the engagement, not the document. Stop there.

**The spine changes.** Not before-and-after. It's now *here's a hard problem, here's how I reasoned through it, here's what shipped.* Your exploration and your rejected concepts carry the whole thing — and they're strong enough to.

### 03 — THE CLIENT  *(~90 words)*
```
Diwa Innovation builds solar-powered air coolers and refrigerators
out of porous terracotta. Founded in Maroua, in Cameroon's Far
North, by an energy engineer who holds a patent on the technology.

The product works on physics rather than electricity: water
evaporating through fired clay pulls heat out of the air. It cools
without a grid connection, in a region where 40°C is a normal
afternoon and the grid is unreliable.

They won first prize at EDF Pulse Africa in Paris, and a
pan-African TotalEnergies award. The engineering had already
been recognised. They came to me to rework the visual identity.
```

**Highlight:** *cools without a grid connection.*

All public. Their dossier and the press coverage cover every sentence.

---

### 04 — THE BRIEF  *(~80 words)*
```
The mark had to do four things at once.

Carry the name — "Diwa" means water, life, and gentleness across
several Lake Chad basin languages. Carry the product — evaporative
cooling, terracotta, sun. Survive one colour, because it prints on
white plastic, brown cardboard, adhesive tape, and vehicle panels.
And hold at favicon size, because the brand lives on WhatsApp
Business as much as anywhere else.

No gradients. No photographic elements. Flat first, everything
else after.
```

This replaces the audit and does the same structural job: it establishes constraints, so the decisions that follow have something to push against.

### 05 — WHAT I DID  *(~40 words)*

Concept development, logo design, colour and type system, and a

### 06 — EXPLORATION  *(~80 words)*
```
Paper first. Four ideas kept coming back: water drop, breeze lines,
leaf, and the letters D and A.

Five made it into vector. Two built from the D, three from the A.
Each drawn as a single flat shape before anything else — if it
doesn't work in one colour at 20 pixels, nothing else about it
matters.

That's where the problem showed up.
```

**Images:**
1. Pencil sheet with `Water drop / Breeze lines / Leaf / D & A` in the margin
2. Second pencil sheet, the refined explorations
3. **The five traced concepts, black and white, full width** — the centrepiece process image

### 07 — KEY DECISIONS

```
CHOSE       Killed three of my own concepts
REJECTED    02, 03, and 04
WHY         They read as flames. 02 as a flame swirl, 03 as fire
            if used at the wrong size, 04 unmistakably. Diwa sells
            cooling in a region where 40°C is normal. A mark that
            reads as fire isn't a stylistic miss — it's the opposite
            of the product. Two of them were the most interesting
            shapes I drew. They still had to go.
```

```
CHOSE       One shape carrying three ideas
REJECTED    Adding elements to signal the product
WHY         The obvious move is to bolt on airflow lines or fan
            blades so people know what's being sold. That makes a
            diagram, not a mark. Instead the D became a water drop,
            a breeze line cuts through it, and that cut forms a leaf
            inside the drop. Water, air, and terracotta ecology —
            one object, no added parts.
```

```
CHOSE       The D as the mark
REJECTED    A separate icon beside the wordmark
WHY         An icon next to a name is two things to maintain and
            two things to misuse. Building the drop into the D means
            the icon and the wordmark are the same object. Pull the
            D out for a favicon and the brand is still legible.
```

```
CHOSE       Merriweather and Inter
REJECTED    System defaults
WHY         Georgia and Calibri read as "document", not "brand".
            Merriweather keeps editorial warmth with proper display
            weights. Inter holds at 12pt on a packaging back panel.
```

**Images:** annotated five-concept sheet (row 1), D-form breakdown slide (row 2), icon files at multiple colours (row 3), type hierarchy page (row 4).

### 08 — THREE FINALISTS  *(~70 words)*
```
Three concepts made it to full lockups, and each had a risk I
wrote down before showing them.

01 — the A as a drop with a leaf inside. Clean, but it could read
as "diw" plus a symbol.
02 — sun, breeze, and a leaflet built from the d. Conceptually the
richest, but it risked reading as "siwa".
03 — the D as drop, breeze, and leaf in one form. No reading
ambiguity, one object, scales cleanly.

I presented two. They chose the D.
```

**Images:** all three lockup slides in order, your "What works / What may not work" annotations visible.

Presenting the risks *before* the client saw them is the detail worth keeping. It shows you don't oversell.

### 09 — THE MARK  *(~50 words)*
```
The D is a water drop. A breeze line cuts through it, and the cut
forms a leaf inside the drop. Three meanings, one shape.

It works in blue, green, ochre, black, or reversed to white. As a
fullmark with the tagline, as a wordmark alone, or as the D on its
own at 20 pixels.
```

**Images:** the D-form breakdown, then the mark across all colourways, then the icon at actual favicon size.

### 10 — THE SYSTEM  *(~55 words)*
```
Three variants — icon, wordmark, fullmark — each with a defined
use and a defined misuse. Four approved colour versions. Clear
space at a quarter of the logo width. Minimum sizes down to
20px. Eight explicit prohibitions, each with a picture, because a
rule without an example gets broken.

Thirty-one pages. Fifteen files. Four formats each.
```

**Images:** variants page, clearspace, minimum size, the eight-rule grid large.

### 13 — WHAT I'D DO DIFFERENTLY  *(~50 words)*

French one instead of leaving a gap the client will eventually
have to fill without me.

## WHAT THIS COSTS YOU, HONESTLY

Losing the before/after is a real loss — visible transformation is the most immediately convincing thing in brand work, and you had a genuine one.

What you keep is arguably more valuable to the right reader. Anyone can show a better logo. Very few designers publish the concepts they killed and the specific reason each one failed. The fire problem, in particular, is evidence of judgment that a before/after slider can't provide.

Clients hire on transformation. Peers and serious clients hire on judgment. This version aims at the second, which suits a portfolio that also sells mentorship.
